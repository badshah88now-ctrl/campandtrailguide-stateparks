Camping in State Parks: Permits, Fees and Rules

By CampAndTrailGuide

State parks are some of the easiest places to camp in the United States. They are close to towns, well maintained, and usually much cheaper than a private campground. But every state runs its own park system, and the rules are not the same everywhere. A little homework before you go saves you a fine, a turned-away arrival, or a night without a site.

Reservations beat first-come, first-served

Most popular state parks now take reservations, and many campgrounds fill weeks ahead on summer weekends. Book through the state's own reservation system — usually the park agency website — and check the cancellation policy before you pay. A few parks still hold some sites for walk-ins, but counting on one at a busy park is a gamble. Arrive with your confirmation saved on your phone, and note the check-in window: many parks lock their gates in the evening, and a late arrival can mean a night in the car park.

Fees, passes, and what they cover

State park camping fees are usually modest — a nightly site fee plus a day-use or vehicle entry fee in some states. An annual state park pass often pays for itself in three or four visits, and it usually covers entry but not the nightly camping fee, so read the fine print. Some states charge extra for hookups, extra vehicles, or a second tent on the same site. Keep cash or a card handy for self-pay stations at parks with no staffed entrance, and keep your receipt on the dashboard or the site post.

Know the rules before you pitch

State parks are stricter than dispersed camping on public land, and rangers do enforce the rules. Quiet hours are real and usually start around 10 pm. Campfires are allowed only in the provided rings or grills, and fire bans can appear overnight in dry weather — check the park's current alerts before you light anything. Pets are welcome in many parks but must stay leashed and out of swimming areas and some trails. Alcohol rules vary by state, and some parks ban it entirely. When in doubt, the park's own website or a quick call to the ranger station is the reliable source.

Plan around the park, not just the site

A state park stay works best when you plan for what is around the campsite, not only the campsite itself. Check which trails start from the campground, whether the lake allows swimming or fishing, and where the nearest drinking water and showers are. Some parks close facilities in the off-season, so a winter trip needs a different plan than a summer one. Book early, respect the rules, and a state park can be the simplest good weekend you will have all year.
